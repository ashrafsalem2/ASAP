# Employment contracts

A contract records what somebody was engaged on, over a stretch of time. It is what payroll reads to decide their wage.

That matters because the wage on an employee's record has no date. It has exactly one value — today's — so a raise entered in April silently restates March: re-run March's payroll and it pays April's figure, with nothing saying the number ever changed, when, or on whose authority.

## One contract per day

For any one person and any one day there is **at most one** contract. Two covering the same day are refused, not resolved.

This is not fussiness. Two contracts covering a day are two wages for that day, and payroll would pay whichever row it read first. The difference does not show up in any total — only in one person's pay, which is exactly where nobody is looking.

## A raise, a promotion, a renewal

Use **supersede**. It closes the current contract the day before the new one begins and records the new one, in one step.

Doing it by hand is where the invariant breaks. Enter the new contract from the first and forget to close the old one, and it is refused for overlapping. Close the old one a day too early and there is a day nobody agreed a wage for.

If the new contract is refused for any reason, the old one is left exactly as it was. Nothing is closed on the strength of a save that did not happen.

## A raise in the middle of a month

The month is split by days between the two figures. Somebody promoted on the sixteenth of a thirty-day month is owed half at the old wage and half at the new, and paying the whole month at either one is wrong by a real amount in somebody's actual pay.

Where somebody also joined or left inside the month, the days actually worked are spread over both contracts in the same proportion. Charging the whole shortfall to one of them would pay the other in full for a stretch nobody worked.

## Permanent, fixed term, probation

A **fixed term** or a **probation** must have an end date; a **permanent** contract must not. Each of those is refused rather than saved, because a fixed term with no end is a permanent contract somebody mislabelled and the difference matters to notice and to end-of-service.

When a fixed term runs out and nothing follows it, payroll says so rather than absorbing it. Somebody being paid past the end of their contract is precisely the thing that goes unnoticed until it has run for months. They are still paid — on the undated figure from their record — and the run tells you which days no contract covered.

## Somebody with no contract yet

Payroll falls back to the wage on their record and warns. That is how the system worked before contracts existed, so nothing stopped working when they arrived — but the warning is worth acting on, because a figure nothing dates will be whatever it says today if that run is ever repeated.

## The figures on the employee card

They are a copy, kept in step with whichever contract is in force **today**. Every screen and report that ever asked what somebody earns reads them, so they stay right. Payroll does not read them when a contract exists, so a copy that fell out of step would be wrong on a card rather than wrong in somebody's pay.
