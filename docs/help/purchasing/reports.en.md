# Purchase reports

Three questions: what am I waiting for, who is letting me down, and where did the money go.

All three are read from what actually happened rather than from a summary kept alongside it. A
delivery date is the posting date of the stock that arrived; a spend figure is what the costing
engine said the goods were worth. Nothing is recorded twice, so nothing can disagree.

## Open orders

What is on order and has not arrived, most overdue first.

An order still waiting for approval is included. Nobody has told the vendor to send anything, but
the person who raised it is still waiting for it, and "what have we got outstanding" is a question
about the business rather than about the paperwork. Cancelled and rejected orders are left out,
because nothing is coming.

Overdue is measured against the date the order promised. An order with no promised date shows a
blank rather than a nought — nobody is late when nobody said when.

## Vendor performance

How each vendor has actually behaved. The arithmetic is trivial; the judgment is not, and two
decisions here decide whether the report tells anybody anything.

**Lateness is averaged over the late deliveries only.** A vendor a fortnight late half the time and
a fortnight early the rest would otherwise come out perfectly punctual. That is the opposite of
useful: an erratic supplier is a worse supplier than a consistently slow one, because nothing can be
planned around them. The consistently slow one you order earlier from.

**A vendor who never promised a date is not scored as on time.** Those deliveries are counted in
their own column and left out of both figures. Counting them as punctual would put the supplier who
refuses to commit to anything at the top of the table.

**One arrival is one delivery, however many lines came on it.** A lorry that turns up on Tuesday
with six items was one delivery. Counting lines would make a vendor's record depend on how the buyer
chose to split the order, which is not a fact about the vendor at all.

Arriving on the promised day is on time. Not before it — a vendor who hits the date exactly every
time is the best supplier there is, and an off-by-one that scored them a day late would be worse
than having no report.

## Purchase analysis

What was bought over a period, by vendor or by item.

Read from what arrived rather than what was ordered. An order is a statement of intent and a good
deal of it never turns into anything; spend is what came through the door and got valued.

The value is the cost the company carries, not the price agreed. Freight landed on a receipt
afterwards is in these figures, because it is part of what those goods cost — which is the whole
reason landed cost exists.

## What is not here yet

**In-full delivery.** Whether a vendor sent everything on the first lorry, as opposed to whether the
first lorry was on time. Both matter and only one is measured.

**Price accuracy.** The three-way match knows when an invoice disagrees with the order; nothing
aggregates that per vendor.

**A promised date that changed.** Nothing stops the expected date being altered, and if it ever can
be, an on-time record measured against it stops meaning anything. Today orders cannot be edited
after release, so the measure is sound — but it is sound by accident rather than by design.
