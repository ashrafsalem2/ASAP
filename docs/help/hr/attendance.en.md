# Attendance and shifts

Attendance records what happened and measures it against what was expected. It does not pay anybody and it does not dock anybody.

That line is the most important thing on this page. A clock showing somebody twenty minutes late is a fact. A deduction for it is a decision, and turning the first into the second automatically would mean the first anybody knew of a new rule was a short payslip. Payroll is told what the attendance says, and somebody then records the leave or the deduction deliberately.

## A shift

A shift is a pattern, not a rota. It says what a normal day looks like for whoever is on it, and that is what lateness and overtime are measured against.

- **Starts and ends.** An end earlier than the start means it crosses midnight. That is the whole definition of a night shift — nothing else distinguishes the two, and a separate switch would be a second thing to keep in step with the times.
- **Break.** Unpaid, and taken off the hours worked. A break longer than the shift is refused: it would leave nothing worked, every day, for as long as nobody noticed.
- **Days of the week.** A shift running on no day is refused. Nobody on it would ever be expected in, so nothing about anybody's absence could ever be said.
- **Grace.** Minutes after the start that are not counted as late. It belongs to the shift rather than to the company because it genuinely differs: a night shift where the handover is at the gate has none, and an office shift where somebody is at their desk by five past has ten and nobody minds.

Lateness is measured from the **start**, not from the end of the grace. Somebody twenty-five minutes late on a ten-minute grace is fifteen minutes late, not five.

## Who is on which shift

One shift per person per day. Putting somebody on a new one closes the previous assignment the day before, so there is neither an overlap nor a day where nothing says what they were expected to work.

That matters historically as much as today. Somebody moved from days to nights in March was late by the night shift's clock in April and by the day shift's clock in February, and one current value could only ever be right about one of those.

Somebody on no shift has their hours recorded and nothing measured. There is no lateness without a start time to be late for, and inventing a nine-to-five to measure against would be inventing a fact.

## Recording a day

One record per person per day. A second is refused; **amend** the one that is there. Two accounts of one day would be added together by every figure derived from them.

Four figures come out of it, and none of them cancels another:

- **Worked** — minutes inside the shift, less the break.
- **Late** — minutes past the start, past the grace.
- **Early leave** — minutes before the shift's end.
- **Overtime** — minutes before the start or after the end.

Somebody twenty minutes late who stays an hour is *twenty minutes late* and *has an hour of overtime*. Netting them to forty would answer no question anybody asked: the manager wants the first figure and the payroll clerk wants the second.

Coming in early does not start the paid day early — the shift is what it is, and a shop paying from the moment somebody arrived would be paying for the queue at the gate — but the time does count as overtime, because it was worked.

## Days off and days of leave

Working a day the shift does not run is **all overtime**, and the record says so. There is no shift to be late for.

Coming in on a day of approved leave is recorded and flagged, not refused. People do come in on days off, and the system has no business telling somebody they did not do what they just did. Shorten the leave if they came back early, or leave both as they are.

## What payroll is told

A day the shift ran, with no attendance record and no approved leave, is **unexplained**. The payroll run reports the count per person and pays them in full for those days.

In full, deliberately. Deciding not to pay somebody is a decision with a name on it, and this screen has no authority to make it. Record the leave, or record the deduction, and the run says so next time.
