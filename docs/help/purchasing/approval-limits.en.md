# Approval limits

Who may sign a purchase order, and for how much.

## The rule that makes signing mean anything

**Nobody may approve an order they raised.** Not when their limit covers it, not when they are the
managing director, not when it is for two hundred riyals.

An approval you can give yourself is not a control, it is a checkbox. The whole point of the step is
that a second person looked, and a system that lets a buyer sign their own order has the paperwork
of an approval process and none of its substance.

That single comparison is the feature. Everything else here is arithmetic around it.

## The threshold

An order worth more than the company's threshold goes for approval instead of to the vendor.
Anything at or below it goes straight out.

At the threshold, not above it — an order for exactly the limit is one somebody chose to let
through, and an off-by-one there is the difference between a rule and a surprise.

Nought means everything is signed for, which is a coherent choice. There is no way to switch
approvals off other than setting the threshold high, and that is deliberate: it leaves the decision
visible as a number somebody chose rather than as a feature nobody turned on.

## Limits are per person

Per person rather than per role, and that is not laziness about roles. An approval limit is a
statement about an individual's authority that somebody signed for, and it is the thing an auditor
asks to see. A role carries it only until somebody is added to the role for an unrelated reason and
quietly gains the authority to sign for a hundred thousand.

Per order, too — not per day and not per vendor. A daily total would mean the same order is
approvable or not depending on what else happened that morning, which is not a rule anybody can plan
around.

**Somebody with no limit at all approves nothing.** Unknown must not mean unlimited, or the answer to
"who can approve this" becomes "whoever has not been set up yet".

## What a refusal tells you

When the approver's limit does not stretch, the refusal names people whose does — excluding whoever
raised the order, because sending it to them would be a dead end.

A refusal that does not name a next step leaves the person holding the order with nowhere to take
it, which is how approval queues turn into email.

## What is recorded

Who signed, when, and **what the order came to at the moment they signed it**.

The amount is frozen with the signature because an approval is authority for an amount, not for an
order number. Anything that changes the total afterwards has to ask again — otherwise a five
thousand order approved on Monday becomes a five hundred thousand order on Tuesday with a signature
still attached to it.

## Rejection

Needs a reason. A rejection with nothing written against it sends the buyer back to guess at what
was wrong, and the order comes round again unchanged.

A rejected order keeps its reason on the record rather than being deleted, because somebody will
ask.

## Who may set limits

Setting approval limits is the authority itself: whoever holds that permission can grant themselves
any amount. It belongs with whoever would sign for the limits on paper, not with whoever maintains
the vendor list.

## What is not here yet

**Approval chains.** One signature releases an order. A company wanting two signatures over a
million, or a route through a department head then finance, cannot express that.

**Re-approval when an order changes.** The approved amount is recorded and compared to nothing yet,
because orders cannot currently be edited after release. The moment they can, the check has to be
wired to it.

**Delegation.** Somebody on leave cannot hand their limit to a deputy for a fortnight.

**A notification.** An order waiting for approval sits there until somebody looks at the list.
