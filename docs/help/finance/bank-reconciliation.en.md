# Bank reconciliation

Two things that look alike and must never be confused. The **ledger account** is what your books say you have. The **bank statement** is what the bank says you have. They disagree constantly and legitimately — a cheque written on the last day of March clears in April — and reconciling is the work of proving that every difference between them is one of those, rather than a mistake or a theft.

## The one piece of arithmetic

Every entry on the bank's ledger account is either one the bank has seen, or one it has not. So your books must be ahead of the bank by exactly the ones it has not seen:

> **ledger balance − bank closing balance = outstanding items**

Everything on this screen exists to make that equation true and visible. When the difference is anything other than nought, something is genuinely wrong and the number itself is usually the clue:

- **Exactly twice an amount you recognise** — a sign the wrong way round. Something was entered as money in when it went out.
- **A multiple of nine** — two digits transposed. 5,481 keyed as 5,841 leaves 360.
- **A round, familiar figure** — often an entry matched to the wrong line, or a payment posted twice.

## The check covers everything, not just this month

Outstanding items are counted against *every* statement ever reconciled, not only the current one. So an entry matched to the wrong line last March throws this month's figure out by the difference.

That is deliberate. An error that stays hidden until somebody happens to look is an error that compounds; one that surfaces at the next month end is an error somebody fixes. It does mean a difference you cannot explain in this month's figures is worth looking for in last month's matches.

## Signs

Lines are entered on the **ledger's** convention: positive is money in, negative is money out.

A bank statement shows a deposit as a credit, because your account is the bank's liability to you. The same deposit debits your cash account. Turn the signs once, when the statement is entered, and never again — reconciliations that flip signs halfway through are how the "exactly twice an amount" difference above gets created.

## Matching

A line is matched to the ledger entry it turned out to be. The system suggests matches where there is exactly one candidate of the right amount within a few days.

Where there are two, it suggests nothing and leaves the line for you. A shop banking the same float twice in a week produces two identical entries, and picking either would be a coin toss dressed up as a decision.

**Matching amounts that differ is allowed**, and warns. One bank line covering a run of three supplier payments is ordinary, and somebody has to be able to record it — match the other two to the same line. What protects the result is the arithmetic at closing, which no amount of loose matching can slip past.

**One entry cannot settle two lines.** The same money did not clear the bank twice, and allowing it would hide a real difference of exactly the same size.

## What usually turns up

**Bank charges and interest.** Real costs nobody keyed, because nobody knew about them until the statement arrived. They appear as statement lines with nothing behind them. Post them, then match.

**Unpresented cheques.** In your books, not yet at the bank. These are the outstanding items and they are correct — the reconciliation closes with them sitting there.

**Deposits in transit.** Banked too late in the day to appear. The same thing, the other way up.

## Closing

A reconciliation closes only when every line is accounted for and the difference is nought. Closing records who agreed it and when, because a reconciliation is a claim somebody is making, not a report somebody ran.

An agreed statement cannot be unpicked. Every later reconciliation was measured against it, so changing a match would make those wrong without anybody being told. A difference found late belongs to the period it was found in — correct it on the current statement, where it can still be explained afterwards.

## Before you start

If the statement's own lines do not add up to the movement between its opening and closing balances, the statement was keyed or imported wrong. No amount of matching will ever close it. This is checked first and said plainly, because the alternative is an afternoon spent looking for the difference in the ledger, where it is not.
