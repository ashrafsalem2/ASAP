# Landed cost

Freight, duty and clearance are part of what the goods cost. This puts them there.

## Why it is not just an expense

A shipping company invoices five thousand for a container. The container held three items at three
different prices, and that five thousand is part of what those goods cost.

Left in an expense account, every margin on those items is overstated for as long as they sell. The
profit on each sale looks better than it was, and the shortfall turns up as a lump in whatever month
the freight invoice happened to land in.

## It has to reach the cost layers

The charge is spread across the receipts it covered and applied per unit received, so subsequent
sales are costed with it. A hundred received at 10.00 with 500 of freight makes the next ten sold
cost 150.00, not 100.00.

That much is the same machinery a revaluation uses.

## The part that makes it harder than a revaluation

**A landed cost is not a decision about future value. It is a correction to what the goods cost all
along.**

Freight that arrives after sixty of a hundred have been sold belongs on all hundred. So:

- the part covering what is still on hand raises **inventory**
- the part covering what has gone corrects **cost of sales**

and the correction lands on the very sales that consumed the receipt, which is what the application
entries are for.

Putting the whole charge into inventory instead is the easy version and it is wrong twice over: the
inventory account carries freight for goods that are not there, and the margin on the sales that
already happened stays overstated for ever.

Where any of the charge reaches goods already sold, the posting says so. A charge quietly landing in
cost of sales is a surprise, and it is the sort somebody notices at the wrong moment.

## By value or by quantity

**By value** is the ordinary choice. Insurance and customs duty follow worth, and the expensive
thing in the container carries more of it. A thousand of duty on 1,000 of one item and 9,000 of
another splits 100 and 900.

**By quantity** is right where the charge follows bulk rather than worth. A pallet fee does not care
that one pallet holds jewellery and the next holds sand.

There is no even split. Spreading a charge equally across dissimilar receipts would be inventing a
basis nobody chose, and the wrong item would carry it.

The shares add up to the charge exactly — the last one carries the rounding, so the entries reconcile
to the invoice rather than to within a halala of it.

## What is refused

**A charge of nothing or less.** A credit note against a charge already landed is a different
operation, and it is not this one.

**No account to post against.** The charge raises the value of goods, and the other side of that has
to be somewhere — the accrual the carrier will be paid from.

**An order with nothing received.** Freight belongs to the goods it carried; until they have arrived
there is nothing to attach it to.

**A basis that comes to nothing.** Nothing to divide by, so nothing can be shared out.

## What is not here yet

**Landed cost as a document.** It is applied as an operation against an order rather than raised as
an item charge line that a vendor invoice can then match against. The three-way match does not see
it.

**Spreading one charge across several orders.** One container holding two purchase orders needs two
postings today, with somebody deciding the split.

**Reversing one.** A landed cost posted in error stays posted; the correction is a manual journal.

**By weight or volume.** The two most physically honest bases for freight, and neither is available
because nothing records a weight.
