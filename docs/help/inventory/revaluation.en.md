# Revaluation

Changing what stock is worth without changing how much of it there is.

## Not an adjustment

An adjustment says there are fewer than the system thought. A revaluation says there are exactly as
many as the system thought and they are worth less.

Damp stock, a supplier credit after the fact, a line nobody will pay full price for again — none of
those are quantity problems. Writing them off as breakage would lose that distinction and make a
shrinkage report say something untrue about how much walked out of the building.

## It has to survive being sold

This is the part that is easy to get wrong, and the failure is silent.

A revaluation posted as a lump sum against nothing in particular puts the write-down into the
inventory account, and leaves the cost layers carrying their old figures. The next sale is then
costed at the price the goods were originally bought at, and the account drifts back towards where
it started. Nothing errors. The only way to notice is to sell the stock and check what it cost.

So the write-down is applied to the open receipts themselves, as an amount per remaining unit. A
hundred received at 10.00 and written down to 6.00 makes the next ten sold cost 60.00, not 100.00,
and what is left is worth 6.00 each.

Where stock came in at several prices, every open receipt lands on the new figure. Otherwise the
cost of a sale would depend on which receipt FIFO happened to reach first.

## Only what is on hand

Goods already sold were costed at what they were worth at the time, and their cost of sales is
already booked against the revenue they earned. A receipt of a hundred with sixty already sold is
revalued on the forty that are left.

Restating what was already sold means reopening a month that is closed. That is a different
operation, and it is not this one.

## Where the loss lands

Against the variance account the item's category names, not cost of goods sold — or against an
account named on the revaluation itself, where a company keeps write-downs separate.

The difference matters. Cost of sales answers "what did the things we sold cost us", and nothing
was sold here. A write-down is a loss on stock still sitting in the building, and putting it in cost
of sales would make the margin on a month look worse for goods that never left it.

An item whose category has no accounts set produces no ledger entries at all. The value entries are
still written and the stock is still revalued; the value simply waits for somebody to say where it
belongs.

## What is refused

**A cost below nothing.** Write it down to nought if it is worthless. Stock carried below nothing is
not a valuation but a liability, and it belongs in a provision somebody can see rather than hidden
inside an inventory balance.

**Revaluing what is not there.** A value with no quantity under it has no receipt to attach to and
would sit in the inventory account as a balance no stock report can explain.

**Nothing is refused for asking twice.** Revaluing stock to what it already costs posts nothing and
says so — an entry for a change of nothing is a row that means nothing.

## What is not here yet

**Revaluing a whole category or location at once.** One item at one location per posting today.

**Standard cost as its own revaluation.** Changing an item's standard cost and revaluing the stock
that was received at the old one are two operations; only the second exists.

**Lower of cost or market as a routine.** The mechanism is here; nothing runs it on a schedule
against a price list.
