# Stock transfers

A transfer moves stock from one location to another in two steps: shipping and receiving.

## Why two steps

Because for the days in between, the stock is in a lorry. Treating a transfer as a single instant would show it arrived the moment it left, and a shop counting its shelves would be short against a system that says otherwise.

Between the two, the stock sits in an in-transit location. It is on the company's books and on nobody's shelves, which is exactly true.

## Cost travels with the goods

The receiving location gets the cost the shipping location released, not a fresh valuation. A transfer is not a sale and does not make a profit; a shop that revalued incoming stock at its own average would book a gain or a loss for moving goods across a car park.

Forty lamps bought at 12 and moved after a delivery at 20 arrive at 480, not 800. The item's purchase cost stays at 20: moving stock says nothing about what it costs to buy.

## Between branches

A transfer between two branches is one transaction with a side in each. The ledger entries carry the branch of the location they moved at, so a transfer out of Jeddah and into Riyadh reads correctly on both branches' figures rather than landing wholly on whichever one happened to be posted from.

## Serials and lots

A specifically costed item names its units when it ships — one serial per unit, or the lot. The transfer keeps them, and receiving takes exactly those units out of transit, each at its own cost. Nobody at the receiving end has to read chassis numbers off a lorry.

Name them at the other end only when not everything arrived:

- **Several serials on a line, some missing:** name the ones that arrived. A quantity alone is refused, because the car left in transit has to be the one that is actually missing.
- **One lot, part of it missing:** key the quantity that arrived; the rest of the lot stays in transit.

## What is refused

Shipping more than is on hand, where the company does not allow negative stock. Receiving more than was shipped, always — the difference is a loss in transit and belongs on an adjustment, not hidden inside a transfer. A tracked line shipped without its numbers, a serial that is not at the source, and a unit named on receipt that is not travelling on the line.
